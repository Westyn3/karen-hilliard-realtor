Selling a Lake Lanier home is a different job than selling a house in a subdivision — because the buyer isn't just buying your house. They're buying your dock, your cove, your water depth, and your slice of the shoreline. Here's what I have waterfront sellers handle before we ever list.

**Get your dock paperwork together.** Your dock permit from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers is part of what you're selling, and serious buyers (and their agents) will ask for it early. Knowing exactly what your permit allows — dock size, slips, any grandfathered features — lets us market those facts confidently instead of scrambling mid-contract. If you're not sure where your permit stands, that's the first thing we sort out. I wrote more about how buyers evaluate this in [my dock permit guide](/blog/dock-permits/).

**Know your water story.** Lanier's level moves with the seasons, and buyers shopping in fall will see the lake lower than it was in May. If your cove holds good water year-round, we prove it — depth at the dock, photos across seasons if you have them. If it doesn't, we price and market honestly, because the buyer will find out anyway.

**Lead with the water, not the kitchen.** Lakefront buyers shop the view first. My waterfront listings get drone photography, water-level shots, and video that shows the dock, the approach by boat, and the sunset — because that's what pulls a buyer off Zillow and into your driveway. Many of Lanier's buyers come from out of state, and the listing has to sell the lifestyle before they ever book a flight.

**Time it deliberately.** There's no single "right" month to sell on Lanier, but there are trade-offs — spring shows the lake at its fullest, while fall and winter buyers tend to be fewer and far more serious. We'll pick the window that fits your situation, not a rule of thumb.
