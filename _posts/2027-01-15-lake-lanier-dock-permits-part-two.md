My first [dock permit guide](/blog/dock-permits/) covered the basics. This one goes deeper, because the dock is one of the most valuable (and most misunderstood) parts of a lake purchase.

**Remember the basics.** The shoreline is managed by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, and dock permits are tied to the property, not to the seller. Not every lakefront lot has one, and not every lot is eligible for one.

**Questions I ask on every lake purchase:**

1. *Is there a current permit, and who holds it?* Get the document, not just a verbal answer.
2. *What does the permit allow?* Dock size, number of slips, and whether features like a lift, a covered area, or a swim platform are included.
3. *Does the dock match the permit?* Sometimes what's physically on the water doesn't match the paperwork. That's a conversation to have before closing, not after.
4. *What's the condition of the dock itself?* Flotation, decking, electrical, and anchoring. A dock inspection is a smart investment.
5. *What's the water depth, and in which seasons?* See [winter pool vs. summer pool](/blog/lake-lanier-winter-pool-vs-summer-pool/).
6. *Are there community rules?* In some gated communities, the association has its own dock guidelines on top of the Corps rules.
7. *Will your boat actually fit?* Slip dimensions and lift capacity should match what you own, or what you plan to buy.

**What a permit can't tell you.** It can't tell you how the water behaves in a dry year, how exposed the cove is, or whether the neighbors' wakes will bother you. That comes from walking the property and knowing the lake.

**A note on the rules themselves.** The Corps' Shoreline Management Plan guides what's allowed on the shoreline, and groups like the [Lake Lanier Association](https://lakelanier.org/) follow it closely on behalf of lake users. Rules can change, so verify current requirements rather than relying on what was true for the last owner.
