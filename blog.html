Before You Buy on Lake Lanier: Dock Permits, Water Depth & Corps Rules

The single most expensive mistake I see lake buyers make is assuming every lakefront lot comes with a dock. It doesn't. On Lake Lanier, the shoreline is managed by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, and dock permits are tied to the property — not to the seller, and not to you. Some lots are permitted, some are grandfathered under older rules, and some will never be dock-eligible because of shoreline classification or water depth.

The second mistake is judging water depth in the wrong season. Lanier's full pool is 1,071 feet, but the lake drops through fall and in drought years, and a cove that looks deep in May can be unusable by October. I look at depth at full pool and at typical winter levels on every property, and I pull the Corps shoreline map before my clients write an offer.

Third: know what the permit allows. Dock size, slip count, and whether a boat lift or platform is permitted vary by permit. If you're buying for a specific boat, that matters. None of this is complicated once you know to ask — but you have to ask before you're under contract, not after.

Milton or Alpharetta? How Luxury Buyers Decide

They share a school cluster, a five-minute drive to Avalon, and a price tag that starts near $1M — so why does the same buyer end up in one and not the other? Almost always, it's land. Milton's zoning preserves large minimum lots across much of the city, which is why you find equestrian estates, barns, and real privacy there. Alpharetta is denser, more walkable, and closer to the tech corridor and downtown.

The practical questions: Do you want a barn or a walk to dinner? Are you commuting to the 400 corridor daily, or working remotely? Do you want a gated golf community like The Manor or White Columns, a lake community like Windward, or acreage on Birmingham Highway? Each answer pulls you toward one city or the other.

My office is in Alpharetta, and I show both every week. The right answer is the one that fits how you'll actually live — and sometimes, after we've toured both, it turns out to be a lake house forty minutes north.

What a Perc Test Is — and Why It Decides What Your Lot Is Worth

Most of North Georgia outside city limits is on septic, not sewer. That means a lot can only support a house if its soil can absorb wastewater at an acceptable rate — and the percolation test, or perc test, is how you find out. A lot that fails, or that only passes for a two-bedroom system, is worth a fraction of one that passes for a five-bedroom home, and they can sit right next to each other.

I came to real estate from construction management, and this is where that background matters most. Before a client buys land, I want the perc results, the well site or water access, the topography, and the road frontage. A steep lot with a long driveway and an engineered foundation can cost six figures more to build on than a flat one with the same view.

If you're buying land in Forsyth, Hall, Dawson, Lumpkin, or Fannin counties, make the perc test a contingency. Every time. It's the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy on a property.
